\section{Special Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge for Sigma Protocols}

\subsection{Definition}

Recall that sigma protocols are three-move interactive proofs of the form
$(R, c, z)$ where $R$ is a commitment (first message), $c$ is a random
challenge from the verifier, and $z$ is the prover's response.  The
important specialisation of HVZK for sigma protocols is called
\emph{special} HVZK.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!60!black,
                  title={Definition: Special Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge}]
A sigma protocol $\mathsf{IP} = \langle \prover, \verifier \rangle$ (with
verification function $\verifyproof$) for language $\lang$ is
\textbf{special honest-verifier zero-knowledge} if there exists a
probabilistic polynomial-time simulator $\Sim$ that takes parameters
$\pp$, input $x$, and challenge $c \in \calC$, and satisfies:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item For $\pp \getsr \Setup$ and all pairs $x, c$, the output of
        $\Sim(\pp, x, c)$ is a proof $\pi = (R, c, z)$ such that
        \[
          \verifyproof(\pp,\, x,\, R,\, c,\, z) = 1.
        \]

  \item For all $(x, w) \in \mathcal{R}_{\lang}$, the transcript $(R, c, z)$
        computed as
        \[
          c \getsr \calC\,;\quad R,\, z \getsr \Sim(x,\, c)
        \]
        is \emph{identically distributed} to a real transcript of the
        interaction $[\prover(\pp, x, w) \leftrightarrow \verifier(\pp, x)]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{remark}
The key distinction of \emph{special} HVZK over plain HVZK is that
the simulator receives the challenge $c$ \emph{before} producing the
commitment $R$.  In a real execution, $R$ is sent first and $c$ is
chosen afterwards; the simulator is allowed to pick $R$ after seeing
$c$, which is what makes efficient simulation possible without knowing
the witness.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Exercise]
Formalize the extension of special HVZK to protocols with
\emph{multiple rounds} (i.e., beyond three moves).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Special HVZK for the Schnorr Protocol}

We now prove that the Schnorr protocol is special HVZK.

Recall the Schnorr protocol for proving knowledge of a discrete
logarithm.  The public parameters are a group $\mathbb{G}$ of prime
order $p$ with generator $G$.  The statement is $X = xG$ (i.e.,
the prover knows $x$), and the verification equation is
\[
  zG = R + cX.
\]

\begin{theorem}
The Schnorr protocol is special HVZK.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We construct the simulator $\Sim(\mathbb{G}, G, p, X, c)$ as follows:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{$\Sim(\mathbb{G}, G, p, X, c)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State $z \getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$
  \State $R \leftarrow zG - cX$
  \State \Return $(R,\, z)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Correctness.}  We verify that the simulated transcript
$(R, c, z)$ passes the verification equation:
\[
  zG \stackrel{?}{=} R + cX.
\]
By construction, $R = zG - cX$, so $R + cX = zG$.  Thus verification
holds with probability 1.

\noindent\textbf{Distribution.}  We must show that the joint distribution
\[
  \bigl[c \getsr \calC\,;\; R, z \getsr \Sim(\mathbb{G}, G, p, X, c)\bigr]
\]
is identically distributed to a real transcript
$[\prover(X, x) \leftrightarrow \verifier(X)]$.

In a real transcript:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The prover picks $r \getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$ and sends $R = rG$.
  \item The verifier sends $c \getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$.
  \item The prover sends $z = r + cx$.
\end{itemize}
So $(c, z)$ are both uniformly random in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ (since $r$ is
uniform and independent of $c$), and $R = zG - cX$ is determined by
$c$ and $z$.

In the simulated transcript:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $z \getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$ is uniform.
  \item $c \getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$ is uniform and independent of $z$.
  \item $R = zG - cX$ is determined by $c$ and $z$.
\end{itemize}

Both distributions produce a uniformly random pair $(c, z) \in
\mathbb{Z}_p^2$ together with $R = zG - cX$.  They are therefore
identically distributed, confirming:
\[
  \bigl[c \getsr \calC\,;\; R, z \getsr \Sim(\mathbb{G}, G, p, X, c)\bigr]
  \;\approx\;
  \bigl[\prover(X, x) \leftrightarrow \verifier(X)\bigr].
\]
\end{proof}

\section{Case Study: Making Sumcheck Zero-Knowledge}

\subsection{Why is Sumcheck \emph{Not} Zero-Knowledge?}

Before constructing a zero-knowledge sumcheck, we recall the plain
sumcheck protocol and identify what information it leaks.

\medskip
\noindent The plain sumcheck protocol for the claim
$\sum_{x_1,\ldots,x_v \in \bits} g(x_1,\ldots,x_v) = H$
(where $g$ is a multilinear polynomial over $\mathbb{F}$) proceeds as
follows.

\medskip
The problem is that the polynomials $g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_v$ sent in
each round are evaluations of $g$ with variables partially fixed to
the random challenges $r_1, \ldots, r_{j-1}$.  These reveal
information about the secret polynomial $g$.  The protocol is
therefore \emph{not} zero-knowledge.

\subsection{High-Level Idea of ZK-Sumcheck}

The approach to making sumcheck zero-knowledge is \emph{commit-and-prove}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hiding commit to the ``leaky'' proof.}
    Instead of sending each round polynomial $g_j$ in the clear, the
    prover sends a \emph{hiding commitment} to $g_j$ (using a
    commitment scheme $\CfCom$, i.e., coefficient-wise commitments to the
    polynomial's coefficients).

  \item \textbf{Prover does verifier's checks ``under commitments.''}
    For each consistency check that the verifier would normally perform
    by evaluating $g_j$, the prover instead provides a zero-knowledge
    proof that the commitment to $g_j$ satisfies the required relation.
    These sub-proofs are called \emph{equality proofs} (EqProof) and
    \emph{polynomial commitment opening proofs} (PC.Open).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{highlight}[title={Key Idea}]
The ZK-sumcheck replaces each plaintext polynomial $g_j$ with a
hiding commitment $C_{g_j} \leftarrow \CfCom(g_j)$.  The verifier
then checks correctness not by inspecting $g_j$ directly, but by
verifying zero-knowledge equality proofs about the commitments.
\end{highlight}

\subsection{Commitment Scheme: \texorpdfstring{$\CfCom$}{CfCom}}

The commitment scheme used is a \emph{coefficient-wise commitment} to
a univariate polynomial.  For a polynomial
\[
  p(X) = a_0 + a_1 X + a_2 X^2 + \cdots
\]
the commitment is
\[
  \CfCom(p) = \bigl(\mathsf{PedCom}(a_0),\, \mathsf{PedCom}(a_1),\, \ldots\bigr),
\]
where $\mathsf{PedCom}(a_i) = a_i G + r_i H$ is a Pedersen commitment
to each coefficient using independent randomness $r_i$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Evaluating under commitments.}  A crucial property of
this scheme is that the commitment to a polynomial evaluated at a
point $r$ can be computed from the coefficient commitments via a
multi-scalar multiplication (MSM).  Specifically, if
$C_{g_j} = (C_{a_0}, C_{a_1}, C_{a_2}, \ldots)$ are the coefficient
commitments for $g_j$, then
\[
  C_{g_j(r_j)} \leftarrow \msm\!\bigl(C_{g_j},\, (1,\, r_j,\, r_j^2,\, \ldots)\bigr).
\]
Similarly, for checking $g_j(0) + g_j(1)$:
\[
  C_{[g_j(0)+g_j(1)]} \leftarrow \msm\!\bigl(C_{g_j},\, (2,\, 1,\, \cdots,\, 1)\bigr).
\]

\noindent\textbf{Illustration.}  For a degree-2 polynomial
$p(X) = a_0 + a_1 X + a_2 X^2$ with commitments
$C_{a_0}, C_{a_1}, C_{a_2}$ (each a Pedersen commitment
$a_i G + r_i H$):
\begin{align*}
  p(0) + p(1) &= a_0 + (a_0 + a_1 + a_2) = 2a_0 + a_1 + a_2,\\
  C_{[p(0)+p(1)]} &= 2(a_0 G + r_0 H) + 1(a_1 G + r_1 H) + 1(a_2 G + r_2 H)\\
                  &= (2a_0 + a_1 + a_2)G + (2r_0 + r_1 + r_2)H.
\end{align*}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Notes on Round 1:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The prover sends $C_{g_1} = \CfCom(g_1)$, a hiding commitment
        to the round-1 polynomial.
  \item The verifier computes a commitment to $g_1(0)+g_1(1)$ via MSM:
        $C_{[g_1(0)+g_1(1)]} \leftarrow \msm(C_{g_1}, (2,1,\cdots,1))$.
  \item The prover runs an $\EqProof$ (a Schnorr-style equality proof)
        to prove that this MSM commitment equals $C_v$ (the commitment
        to the claimed sum $H$).  This ensures $g_1(0)+g_1(1) = H$
        without revealing $g_1$.
  \item The verifier samples $r_1 \getsr \mathbb{F}$ and computes
        $C_{g_1(r_1)} \leftarrow \msm(C_{g_1}, (1, r_1, r_1^2, \ldots))$.
        This will be used as the ``target'' for the next round.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Notes on the Final Round:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the last round, the prover sets
        $g_v \leftarrow g(r_1,\ldots,r_{v-1}, X_v)$ and commits
        to it.
  \item The consistency check (that $g_v(0)+g_v(1) = g_{v-1}(r_{v-1})$)
        is again handled with an $\EqProof$ under commitments.
  \item In place of the plain oracle query $g_v(r_v) = g(r_1,\ldots,r_v)$,
        the prover provides a \textbf{polynomial commitment opening proof}:
        \[
          \PCOpen\bigl(C_g,\,(r_1,\ldots,r_v),\,C_{g_v(r_v)}\bigr).
        \]
        This proves that the committed polynomial $g$ (committed in $C_g$)
        evaluates to the same value as $g_v(r_v)$ at the random point
        $(r_1,\ldots,r_v)$, without revealing $g$ or its evaluation.
  \item The simulator for this protocol is
        $\Sim(C_g, C_v, (r_1, \ldots, r_n))$ (see ZK property
        discussion below).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{ZK Property of the ZK-Sumcheck}

The ZK property follows from the composition of the hiding properties
of the commitment scheme and the HVZK properties of the sub-proofs
(EqProof and PC.Open).  Intuitively:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The commitments $C_{g_j}$ hide $g_j$ by the hiding property
        of $\CfCom$.
  \item The equality proofs are Schnorr-type protocols, which are
        special HVZK (as shown in Section~2).
  \item The polynomial commitment opening proof is also HVZK.
\end{itemize}
A simulator for the full ZK-sumcheck protocol is
$\Sim(C_g, C_v, (r_1,\ldots,r_v))$: it simulates each round's
commitment and equality proof without knowing $g$ or $v$.

\section{Getting to NIZKs via Fiat-Shamir}

\subsection{Background}

Interactive proof systems require the prover and verifier to
communicate.  For many applications (e.g., blockchain, digital
signatures) we want \emph{non-interactive} zero-knowledge proofs
(NIZKs) that can be verified without back-and-forth communication.

The \textbf{Fiat-Shamir (FS) transform} converts an interactive
protocol into a non-interactive one by replacing the verifier's
random challenges with outputs of a cryptographic hash function
(modeled as a \emph{random oracle}).

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!60!black,
                  title={Fiat-Shamir Transform}]
Let $\mathsf{IP} = \langle \Setup, \prover, \verifier \rangle$ be an interactive
argument.  The \textbf{Fiat-Shamir transform} with random oracle
$\RO$ produces a non-interactive argument
\[
  \mathsf{FS[IP]} = \bigl(\Setup,\; \mathsf{FS[IP]}.\prover^{\RO},\;
  \mathsf{FS[IP]}.\verifier^{\RO}\bigr),
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The prover $\mathsf{FS[IP]}.\prover^{\RO}$ runs the
        interactive prover but replaces each verifier challenge with
        $c_i = \RO(\text{transcript so far})$.
  \item The verifier $\mathsf{FS[IP]}.\verifier^{\RO}$ recomputes
        all challenges using $\RO$ and checks the proof.
\end{itemize}
The resulting proof $\pi$ is a static object (a string) that any
verifier can check without interaction.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{``Fiat-Shamir Preserves ZK''}

\begin{theorem}[\textbf{FS Preserves ZK}]
Let $\mathsf{IP} = \langle \Setup, \prover, \verifier \rangle$ be an interactive
argument for language $\lang$.  Let
\[
  \mathsf{FS[IP]} = \bigl(\Setup,\; \mathsf{FS[IP]}.\prover^{\RO},\;
  \mathsf{FS[IP]}.\verifier^{\RO}\bigr)
\]
be the non-interactive argument obtained from applying the Fiat-Shamir
transform with random oracle $\RO$ to $\mathsf{IP}$.  If
$\mathsf{IP}$ is \emph{special honest-verifier zero-knowledge} and
has \emph{unpredictable commitments}, then $\mathsf{FS[IP]}$ is a
\textbf{non-interactive zero-knowledge argument}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (sketch)]
We need to construct a simulator $\Sim^{\RO}$ for
$\mathsf{FS[IP]}$ that, given only the statement $x$, produces a
simulated proof $\pi$ that is indistinguishable from a real proof, in
the random oracle model.

The simulator proceeds as follows (exploiting programmability of the
random oracle):

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{NIZK Simulator $\Sim^{\RO}(x)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State // We simulate the NIZK proof for statement $x$
  \State // Key idea: we can \emph{program} the random oracle $\RO$
  \State Pick a random challenge $c \getsr \calC$
  \State Run the HVZK simulator: $(R, z) \getsr \Sim_{\mathsf{IP}}(x, c)$
  \State Program the random oracle: set $\RO(R) := c$
  \State \Return $\pi = (R, c, z)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Why this works:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The HVZK simulator $\Sim_{\mathsf{IP}}$ produces a
        transcript $(R, c, z)$ that is identically distributed to a
        real transcript (by special HVZK), where $c$ is chosen before
        $R$ is generated.
  \item In the non-interactive setting, $c = \RO(R)$; since the
        simulator can program the random oracle, it sets
        $\RO(R) = c$.
  \item The \emph{unpredictable commitments} property ensures that $R$
        was not previously queried to $\RO$ by the adversary
        (with overwhelming probability), so there is no conflict in
        programming $\RO(R) = c$.
  \item The resulting simulated proof $\pi = (R, c, z)$ is
        indistinguishable from a real proof:
        \begin{align*}
          &\text{Real proof: } \prover(x, w) \text{ with } c = \RO(R) \\
          &\text{Simulated proof: } \Sim_{\mathsf{IP}}(x, c),\
            \RO(R) := c.
        \end{align*}
\end{itemize}

\noindent The simulator picks $c$ first, generates $(R,z)$ via
$\Sim_{\mathsf{IP}}$, and programs $\RO(R)=c$ to make the
hash consistent.  This is valid because:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The HVZK distribution is the same whether $c$ is chosen before
        or after $R$ (this is the whole point of special HVZK).
  \item Unpredictable commitments ensure the random oracle at $R$
        has not been queried before, so programming is safe.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The condition \emph{unpredictable commitments} (sometimes called
``unique commitments'' or ``high-entropy commitments'') means that
the prover's first message $R$ is computationally unpredictable ---
i.e., given only the statement $x$, no PPT adversary can guess $R$
before the prover generates it.  For Schnorr, $R = rG$ with $r
\getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$ is unpredictable under the discrete logarithm
assumption.
\end{remark}

\section{Practical Considerations for Zero Knowledge}

In practice, when deploying zero-knowledge proof systems there are
several important considerations.

\begin{highlight}[title={Practical Considerations}]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Choice of commitment scheme.}
    The ZK-sumcheck construction requires a \emph{hiding} and
    \emph{binding} commitment scheme.  Pedersen commitments over
    elliptic curves are a standard choice: $\mathsf{Com}(v; r) = vG + rH$
    for independently chosen generators $G, H$.

  \item \textbf{Polynomial commitment schemes.}
    The final round of ZK-sumcheck requires a polynomial commitment
    opening proof (PC.Open).  In practice, schemes such as KZG,
    FRI, or IPA are used, each with different trade-offs in proof size,
    prover time, and verifier time.

  \item \textbf{Efficiency of EqProof.}
    Equality proofs between Pedersen commitments are efficient
    Schnorr-type protocols.  They add only a constant overhead per
    round of sumcheck.

  \item \textbf{Non-interactive proofs via Fiat-Shamir.}
    The Fiat-Shamir transform (applied to the ZK-sumcheck and
    sub-protocols) converts the whole interactive argument into a
    NIZK suitable for blockchain and other settings.  The random
    oracle is instantiated with a cryptographic hash function (e.g.,
    SHA-3 or Poseidon in the arithmetic setting).

  \item \textbf{Proof size and verification cost.}
    Each round of ZK-sumcheck sends one commitment $C_{g_j}$ plus
    one EqProof.  For $v$ variables and degree-$d$ polynomials per
    round, the total proof consists of $O(v \cdot d)$ group elements.

  \item \textbf{The role of the random oracle model.}
    The security of Fiat-Shamir NIZKs is proven in the random oracle
    model (ROM).  In practice, the hash function must be modeled
    carefully; the proof fails in general if the hash function has
    structure that the adversary can exploit.
\end{itemize}
\end{highlight}

% Summary
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section*{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Special HVZK} strengthens plain HVZK by requiring a
        simulator that receives the challenge \emph{before} producing
        the commitment.  This is the right notion for sigma protocols
        and is what Fiat-Shamir exploits.

  \item The \textbf{Schnorr protocol} is special HVZK.  The simulator
        $\Sim(\mathbb{G}, G, p, X, c)$ picks $z \getsr \mathbb{Z}_p$,
        computes $R = zG - cX$, and returns $(R, z)$.  The distribution
        of $(R, c, z)$ is identical to a real transcript.

  \item The \textbf{plain sumcheck} is not ZK because the round
        polynomials $g_j$ reveal information about $g$.  The
        \textbf{ZK-sumcheck} fixes this via a commit-and-prove
        approach: the prover commits to each $g_j$ using $\CfCom$
        and replaces verifier checks with equality proofs and a
        polynomial commitment opening in the final round.

  \item \textbf{Fiat-Shamir preserves ZK}: if an interactive argument
        is special HVZK and has unpredictable commitments, then the
        Fiat-Shamir transform produces a NIZK (in the random oracle
        model).  The NIZK simulator programs the random oracle to make
        the simulated commitment consistent with the simulated challenge.

  \item In practice, ZK-sumcheck is combined with Fiat-Shamir to
        yield efficient NIZKs for multilinear polynomial sum claims,
        used as building blocks in systems such as Spartan, Lasso,
        and others.
\end{itemize}
